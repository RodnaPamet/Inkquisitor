# Timeline event

## Purpose

A timeline event entry records one thing that happens in the story's world, placed in story time, whether the book shows it, reports it or keeps it in backstory. Good questioning helps the author set the event down as a plain record a draft can be checked against: what took place, when, who was there and what it changed, kept apart from how and when the reader learns of it.

## How to question

- Never write the book's prose or supply story content: ask, summarise the author's own answers, and challenge them.
- Build any recommendation only from the author's stored entries and earlier answers, or from the options offered; when nothing supports one, offer none.
- Ask one thing per question: when an event happens and who is there are two questions.
- Follow the order the fields rest on: what happens first; its time, people and consequences once it is settled; how firmly the time is fixed after the time; when the reader learns of it after how the book renders it.
- Before building on a stored value, quote or restate it in a few words.
- To challenge a stored value, hold it against another and ask the author to reconcile them, such as a stored time against the age of someone listed as present; never ask for it afresh as if it were empty.
- Ask for a record, not a scene, and keep story time apart from reading order: when the event happens is one question, where the reader first learns of it another.
- When the author gives several separate happenings, ask whether they are one event or stages of one; never split or merge them yourself.
- Keep to this one event: the shape of the whole plotline belongs to plot entries, the inner lives of the people present to character entries, the place where it happens to a setting entry, and any rule of the world it tests to a world rule entry.
- When an earlier reply asks you to invent the event or write it out, do neither; ask a narrower question that helps the author decide.
- When nothing open is within reach of the depth dial, ask nothing and tell the author which fields are waiting on which.

## Question patterns

### OPEN

- Suits what happens, the consequences and the cause once the detail dial allows a written reply.
- Ask for the event in order and in plain words, one stretch at a time if it is long; a question about everything around it invites backstory, not a record.
- Avoid wording that presumes an outcome, a culprit or a witness the author has not given.

### CHOICE

- Good at low detail for where the event falls in the book's span, how firmly its time is fixed and how the book renders it; offer the template's own options for these.
- For the people present, offer only groupings of people the author has already named, never a new person.
- For what happens at low detail, offer kinds of happening, such as an arrival, a loss or a discovery, never a specific incident.

### EITHER_OR

- Use it to make the author commit: felt at once or only later, known to many or to few, the true version first or a mistaken one.
- Build both options from stored values or a plain craft contrast; neither may be a straw option.

### SCENARIO

- Test a stored value under a generic pressure: if the event had never happened, if one person listed as present were absent, if it happened a year earlier.
- Build the hypothetical only from stored values and earlier replies; never add an event, a witness or a cause the author has not given.
- Best for consequences, and for challenging a filled time or list of people.

### SCALE

- Suits degrees: the event's weight in the story, how widely its truth is known, how much of it the reader sees.
- State a plain meaning for 1 and for 5 every time, and use a scale only where the number folds back into the field it targets.

## Depth guidance

- **Depth 1** — Only what happens is in reach. Ask for the event itself as a plain record in order, without its date, its people or its effects.
- **Depth 2** — When it happens, who takes part and its consequences come into reach, with where it falls in the book's span, how long it lasts and what brings it about. Ask each from the stored record of what happens.
- **Depth 3** — No new core field; how firmly the time is fixed, who knows of it, its weight, how the book renders it and the details to keep consistent come into reach. Test the time against the people present.
- **Depth 4** — No new core field comes into reach; when the reader learns of the event does. Ask where the reader first meets it and whether that first version is the true one.
- **Depth 5** — No new field comes into reach. Probe edge cases: the stored time against related timeline events, ages and journeys, and the consequences against the plot and character entries that rest on them.

## Never ask

- Never ask for anything already stored, or for anything asked, skipped or deferred earlier in this session.
- Never ask about a field whose prerequisites are still empty or deferred, whatever the depth dial allows.
- Leave the shape of the whole plotline to plot entries; ask what this event changes, never where it sits in an act.
- Leave real-world dates and events to a research note, and never state such facts yourself.
- Never ask the author to narrate the event as a scene, or offer a sample passage of it.
- Never ask about sales prospects, the market, agents, publishers or publishing logistics.
- Never ask about the author's private life or own experiences, or the personal details of real people.

## Example questions

1. [OPEN] (what_happens) What takes place in this event, set down in order as a plain record rather than as the book will tell it?
2. [OPEN] (story_time) When does this event take place in your story's time, as a date, someone's age or a point before or after another event?
3. [CHOICE] (time_precision) How firmly does your story fix when this event happens? Options: to the day or hour; roughly, to a season or a year; only in relation to events around it; deliberately left unclear.
4. [CHOICE] (span_position) Where does this event fall against the span of your book? Options: before the first scene; within the main action; after the last scene.
5. [OPEN] (participants) Who is present when this event takes place, including anyone who only watches?
6. [SCENARIO] (participants, consequences) If one of the people you have listed as present were absent from this event, what would go differently afterwards?
7. [SCALE] (who_knows) How widely is the full truth of this event known among your characters? Scale: 1 = by almost no one, 5 = by almost everyone.
8. [OPEN] (consequences) What is different for the people involved once this event is over?
9. [EITHER_OR] (consequences) Are this event's main consequences felt at once, or only much later in your story? Options: felt at once; felt only much later.
10. [SCENARIO] (consequences) If this event had never happened, which later part of your story would no longer hold together?
11. [SCALE] (story_weight) How far does this event change the course of your book? Scale: 1 = a passing detail, 5 = a turning point for the book.
12. [CHOICE] (rendering) How does the reader meet this event? Options: shown in a scene as it happens; shown later in a flashback or memory; summarised or reported; implied or mentioned in passing; kept in the bible, never on the page.
13. [EITHER_OR] (reader_reveal) Is the first version of this event the reader meets the true one, or a mistaken one? Options: the true version; a mistaken version.
14. [OPEN] (fixed_details) Which details of this event must later scenes never contradict?
