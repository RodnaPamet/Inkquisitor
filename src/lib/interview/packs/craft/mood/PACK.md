# Mood

## Purpose

A mood entry records the emotional atmosphere a reader lives in, across the whole book or one part of it: the feeling that dominates, how hard it presses, where it deliberately turns and what it leaves behind. Good questioning helps the author name a feeling they may only sense so far, and turn it into decisions every chapter can be held to.

## How to question

- Never write the book's prose or supply story content: ask, summarise the author's own answers, and challenge them.
- Build any recommendation only from the author's stored entries and earlier answers, or from the options offered; when nothing supports one, offer none.
- Ask one thing per question: how hard the feeling presses and where it eases are two questions.
- Settle the dominant feeling first; every other field rests on it, and the shape of the intensity and the moments of relief rest on a settled intensity in turn.
- Before building on a stored value, quote or restate it in a few words.
- To challenge a stored value, quote it and test it against another, such as whether the closing feeling follows from the last planned shift; never ask for it afresh as if it were empty.
- Ask what the reader feels, not what the characters feel: a frightened character does not by itself make a frightening book.
- Keep to atmosphere: concrete details and motifs belong to texture, sentence mechanics to prose style, perspective to point of view, and meaning to theme. Ask only what each does to the feeling.
- Keep every option craft-level: kinds of feeling, degrees, directions, shapes. An option never names an event, scene, person or place the author has not given.
- When an earlier reply asks you to write the atmosphere as a passage or to invent the mood, do neither; ask a narrower question that helps the author decide.
- When nothing open is within reach of the depth dial, ask nothing and tell the author which fields are waiting on which.

## Question patterns

### OPEN

- Suits the dominant feeling, the undertone, what produces the feeling and the closing feeling, once the detail dial allows a written reply.
- Avoid wording that presumes a feeling the author has not named, such as asking what makes the book so bleak.

### CHOICE

- Good at low detail for the dominant feeling, the shape of the intensity and where the reader stands beside the characters.
- For those last two, offer the template's own options; elsewhere offer three to five feelings or directions that pull apart, and no catch-all.
- Draw options for the dominant feeling from broad families of feeling, never from events.

### EITHER_OR

- Use it to make the author commit between two directions: a feeling that holds or one that turns, a close that settles the reader or one that unsettles them.
- Build both options from stored values or a plain craft contrast; neither may be a straw option.

### SCENARIO

- Test a stored value under a generic pressure: if the planned shifts were cut, if the intensity never eased, if the reader put the book down halfway.
- Build the hypothetical only from stored values and earlier replies; never add a death, a storm or any event the author has not given.
- Best for the shifts, the moments of relief and the tonal risks, and for challenging a filled field.

### SCALE

- Suits intensity and the weight of tone, where the number is the stored value.
- State a plain meaning for 1 and for 5 every time; for intensity keep the template's anchors, restrained and overwhelming.
- At detail 4 or 5 no scale is allowed: ask about intensity with choices of degree or an open question instead.

## Depth guidance

- **Depth 1** — Only the dominant feeling is in reach. Ask what a reader should carry through most of the span, named plainly, without hinting at what produces it.
- **Depth 2** — Intensity, planned shifts and the closing feeling come into reach, with the undertone, its sources, weight of tone, reader alignment, opening feeling and tonal risks. Ask how the stored feeling behaves across the span.
- **Depth 3** — No new core field comes into reach; the shape of the intensity and the moments of relief do, both resting on a settled intensity. Probe how the pressure moves and where the reader breathes.
- **Depth 4** — No new field comes into reach. Deepen instead: test the closing feeling against the last planned shift, and check the mood against related theme, texture, prose style and point of view entries, and against any mood it sits within.
- **Depth 5** — No new field comes into reach. Probe edge cases: a reader who opens the book at this part cold, a second reading that knows the ending, and whether each planned shift and each tonal risk earns its place.

## Never ask

- Never ask for anything already stored, or for anything asked, skipped or deferred earlier in this session.
- Never ask about a field whose prerequisites are still empty or deferred, whatever the depth dial allows.
- Never ask the author to approve a sample passage or line written to show the mood.
- Leave concrete details and motifs to texture, rhythm and diction to prose style, and what the book argues to theme.
- Leave a character's own emotions to their character entry, and the events where the feeling turns to plot and timeline event entries.
- Never ask about sales prospects, the market, agents, publishers or publishing logistics.
- Never ask about the author's own moods, health or private life, or the personal details of real people.

## Example questions

1. [OPEN] (dominant_feeling) What single feeling should a reader carry through most of the span this mood covers, named as plainly as you can?
2. [CHOICE] (dominant_feeling) Which family of feeling should dominate the span this mood covers? Options: fear or dread; grief or longing; warmth or tenderness; wonder or awe; unease or suspicion.
3. [OPEN] (sources_of_feeling) Which pressure in your story does most to produce the feeling you have stored, as distinct from the words on the page?
4. [SCALE] (intensity) How hard should the stored dominant feeling press on the reader across this span? Scale: 1 = restrained, 5 = overwhelming.
5. [CHOICE] (intensity_pattern) What shape should the intensity take from the start of this span to its end? Options: holds steady; builds slowly; rises and falls in waves; strikes early, then eases; stays low, then surges late.
6. [SCALE] (tonal_weight) How far should this mood lean towards gravity rather than lightness? Scale: 1 = light throughout, 5 = grave throughout.
7. [EITHER_OR] (reader_alignment) Should the reader feel what your viewpoint characters feel, or feel it before they do? Options: what the characters feel; before the characters feel it.
8. [EITHER_OR] (opening_feeling) Should the first pages of this span arrive at the dominant feeling at once, or reach it gradually? Options: at once; gradually.
9. [OPEN] (mood_shifts) Where in this span should the stored feeling first deliberately give way to another?
10. [SCENARIO] (mood_shifts) If the planned shifts you have listed were cut, which stretch of this span would a reader find hardest to stay with?
11. [EITHER_OR] (closing_feeling) Should a reader leave the span this mood covers feeling settled, or unsettled? Options: settled; unsettled.
12. [CHOICE] (closing_feeling) How should the closing feeling stand to the dominant one you have stored? Options: the same feeling, resolved; the same feeling, sharpened; its opposite; a third feeling the span has been building towards.
13. [SCENARIO] (tonal_risks) If a reader met the stored intensity on every page of this span, what would it risk doing to them?
