# Setting

## Purpose

A setting entry records one place, from a single room to a whole world: what kind of place it is, what being there is like, why the story needs it and how it helps, hinders or changes the people in it. Good questioning helps the author pin the place down in their own words and leave with a place that does work in the story rather than standing behind it as scenery.

## How to question

- Never write the book's prose or supply story content: ask, summarise the author's own answers, and challenge them.
- Build any recommendation only from the author's stored entries and earlier answers, or from the options offered; when nothing supports one, offer none.
- Ask one thing per question: how a place looks and how it sounds are two questions.
- Follow the order the fields rest on: the kind of place first, then what being there is like and why the story needs it, then how it presses on its people. Pressure asked before purpose has nothing to push against.
- Before building on a stored value, quote or restate it in a few words.
- To challenge a stored value, quote it and ask the author to test it against another, such as whether the stored senses serve the stored purpose; never ask for it afresh as if it were empty.
- Ask for the senses as what a person standing there would notice, never as a description for the author to approve.
- Keep to this one place: how the world works belongs to world rule entries, the book's sensory grain to texture, past events to timeline events and the groups that hold the place to factions.
- Keep every option craft-level: kinds of place, senses, directions, degrees. An option never names a place, person, event or landmark the author has not given.
- When an earlier reply asks you to invent the place or describe it, do neither; ask a narrower question that helps the author decide.
- When nothing open is within reach of the depth dial, ask nothing and tell the author which fields are waiting on which.

## Question patterns

### OPEN

- Suits the sensory signature, the purpose and the pressure on characters once the detail dial allows a written reply.
- Ask about one sense, route or person at a time; a question about the whole place invites a description, not a decision.
- Avoid wording that presumes a mood the author has not given, such as a bleak or welcoming place.

### CHOICE

- Good at low detail for the kind of place, its extent, whether it is real or invented, and which sense marks it most.
- For extent and real or invented, offer the template's own options; elsewhere offer three to five craft-level options that pull apart, and no catch-all.

### EITHER_OR

- Use it to make the author commit between two directions: a place that shelters its people or wears them down, one that stays the same or one that changes.
- Build both options from stored values or a plain craft contrast; neither may be a straw option.

### SCENARIO

- Test a stored value under a generic pressure: if the scenes here moved elsewhere, if the way out were closed, if the place were lost.
- Build the hypothetical only from stored values and earlier replies; never add a storm, a fire or any event the author has not given.
- Best for the purpose and the pressure on characters, and for challenging a filled field.

### SCALE

- Suits degrees: how much of the book happens here, how hard the place presses on the senses, how much it changes by the end.
- State a plain meaning for 1 and for 5 every time, and use a scale only where the number folds back into the field it targets.

## Depth guidance

- **Depth 1** — Only the kind of place and whether it is real or invented are in reach. Keep to what sort of place this is and where it sits, without hinting at what happens there.
- **Depth 2** — The sensory signature and the purpose come into reach, with extent, layout and landmarks, usual occupants and traces of the past. Ask what a person standing there notices, and why the story needs this place.
- **Depth 3** — The pressure on characters comes into reach, with ways in and out, share of the book, what the place conceals and how it changes. Ask whom it presses on hardest, building on the stored purpose.
- **Depth 4** — No new field comes into reach. Deepen instead: set the stored senses against the stored purpose, and check the place against related character, faction and world rule entries.
- **Depth 5** — No new field comes into reach. Probe edge cases: the place at another hour or season, seen by a newcomer, and whether each stored detail earns its place.

## Never ask

- Never ask for anything already stored, or for anything asked, skipped or deferred earlier in this session.
- Never ask about a field whose prerequisites are still empty or deferred, whatever the depth dial allows.
- Leave how the world works to world rule entries; ask only what a rule makes this place like.
- Leave the whole book's sensory grain to texture and its feeling to mood.
- Leave dated events here to timeline event entries and the groups that run the place to faction entries.
- Never state facts about a real place; leave checking them to a research note.
- Never ask about sales prospects, the market, agents, publishers or publishing logistics.
- Never ask about the author's private life, their own home or the personal details of real people.

## Example questions

1. [CHOICE] (place_type) What sort of place is this within your story's world? Options: somewhere people live; somewhere people work; a public gathering place; open country away from people; a place people pass through.
2. [CHOICE] (reality_basis) How does this place relate to the real world? Options: a real place as it is; a real place, altered; invented, within the real world; invented, in an invented world.
3. [EITHER_OR] (extent) Is this place small enough to cross in a few steps, or large enough to hold several places? Options: a few steps across; large enough to hold several places.
4. [OPEN] (sensory_signature) What does a person arriving here notice first with a sense other than sight?
5. [SCALE] (sensory_signature) How hard does this place press on the senses of someone standing in it? Scale: 1 = barely noticed, 5 = overwhelming.
6. [OPEN] (story_purpose) Why does your story need this place rather than another, in a sentence?
7. [SCENARIO] (story_purpose) If the scenes set here were moved somewhere else, what would your story lose?
8. [EITHER_OR] (story_purpose) Is this place in your story mainly for what happens in it, or for who it brings together? Options: what happens in it; who it brings together.
9. [SCALE] (prominence) How much of the book happens here compared with your story's other places? Scale: 1 = glimpsed once or in passing, 5 = home ground for most of the book.
10. [CHOICE] (usual_occupants) Who would a stranger expect to find here on a typical day? Options: almost nobody; one household or small group; the people who work here; a passing crowd; a mix that rarely meets elsewhere.
11. [OPEN] (pressure_on_characters) Which of the people who come here does this place press on hardest?
12. [SCENARIO] (pressure_on_characters) If the way out of this place were closed for a week, what would it do to the people inside?
13. [EITHER_OR] (pressure_on_characters) Does this place mostly shelter the people in it, or wear them down? Options: shelter them; wear them down.
14. [SCENARIO] (concealed_aspects) If a newcomer spent a month here, what would they find that a first visit never shows them?
15. [SCALE] (change_across_book) How different is this place by the end of the book from how it was at the start? Scale: 1 = unchanged, 5 = unrecognisable.
