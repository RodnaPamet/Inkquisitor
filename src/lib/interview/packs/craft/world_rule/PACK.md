# World rule

## Purpose

A world rule entry records one way the story's world works, in magic, technology, law, custom or nature: what the rule says, what it costs, where it stops, who knows it and how it bends events. Good questioning helps the author state the rule plainly enough to check a draft against, and find the cost and the edge that make it cause trouble for characters rather than solve it for them.

## How to question

- Never write the book's prose or supply story content: ask, summarise the author's own answers, and challenge them.
- Build any recommendation only from the author's stored entries and earlier answers, or from the options offered; when nothing supports one, offer none.
- Ask one thing per question: what the rule costs and who knows it are two questions.
- Follow the order the fields rest on: the statement first, then its cost, limits and who knows it, then what it does to events. Consequences asked before the cost is known have nothing to grow from.
- Before building on a stored value, quote or restate it in a few words.
- To challenge a stored value, quote it and test it against another, such as whether the stored cost would stop anyone using the rule; never ask for it afresh as if it were empty.
- Press on cost and limits: a rule with no price and no edge tends to settle the plot for whoever holds it. Ask where the author's rule stops; never propose where it should.
- Ask for the statement as one sentence a reader could hold a scene to, not for the lore around it.
- Keep to this one rule: places belong to setting entries, groups that enforce or contest it to faction entries, events that establish or test it to timeline event entries, and real-world facts it draws on to research notes.
- Keep every option craft-level: domains, degrees, kinds of cost, kinds of knower. An option never names a power, place, group or event the author has not given.
- When an earlier reply asks you to invent the rule or write a passage, do neither; ask a narrower question that helps the author decide.
- When nothing open is within reach of the depth dial, ask nothing and tell the author which fields are waiting on which.

## Question patterns

### OPEN

- Suits the statement, the cost and limits and the consequences once the detail dial allows a written reply.
- Ask about one cost, one limit or one group of knowers at a time; a question about the whole system invites lore, not a decision.
- Avoid wording that presumes a cost, a source or a loophole the author has not given.

### CHOICE

- Good at low detail for the domain, who the rule applies to, what keeps it in force and which kind of cost it carries.
- For who it applies to and what keeps it in force, offer the template's own options; elsewhere offer three to five craft-level options that pull apart, and no catch-all.

### EITHER_OR

- Use it to make the author commit: known to most or to few, a cost paid by the user or by others, a rule that holds to the end or one that fails.
- Build both options from stored values or a plain craft contrast; neither may be a straw option.

### SCENARIO

- Test a stored value under a generic pressure: if the cost were waived, if the rule were broken in public, if someone who misunderstands it tried to use it.
- Build the hypothetical only from stored values and earlier replies; never add a war, a discovery or any event the author has not given.
- Best for cost and limits and for consequences, and for challenging a filled field.

### SCALE

- Suits degrees: how absolute the rule is, how much the reader is told, how widely it is understood.
- State a plain meaning for 1 and for 5 every time, and use a scale only where the number folds back into the field it targets.

## Depth guidance

- **Depth 1** — Only the statement and the domain are in reach. Ask for the rule as one checkable sentence and the area of the world it belongs to, without hinting at its cost.
- **Depth 2** — Cost and limits and who knows come into reach, with who it applies to, its source, what keeps it in force, how absolute it is and how much the reader is told. Ask what it costs, where it stops and who understands it.
- **Depth 3** — Consequences for the story come into reach, with how the reader learns the rule and its effect on everyday life. Ask which choices it forces or forbids, building on the stored cost.
- **Depth 4** — No new core field comes into reach; continuity risks and change across the book do. Ask where a draft could slip against the rule and whether the rule holds to the end.
- **Depth 5** — No new field comes into reach. Probe edge cases: the rule at its limit, in the hands of someone who misreads it, and against related world rule, faction and character entries.

## Never ask

- Never ask for anything already stored, or for anything asked, skipped or deferred earlier in this session.
- Never ask about a field whose prerequisites are still empty or deferred, whatever the depth dial allows.
- Leave places to setting entries; ask what the rule makes possible or impossible there, never what a place is like.
- Leave the groups that enforce or contest the rule to faction entries, and the events that establish or test it to timeline event entries.
- Leave real-world science, law and history to a research note, and never state such facts yourself.
- Never ask about sales prospects, the market, agents, publishers or publishing logistics.
- Never ask about the author's private life or own beliefs, or the personal details of real people.

## Example questions

1. [OPEN] (rule_statement) How would you state this rule in one plain sentence that a reader could hold a scene to?
2. [CHOICE] (domain) Which area of your story's world does this rule belong to? Options: magic; technology; law; custom; nature.
3. [OPEN] (cost_or_limit) What does using this rule cost the person who uses it?
4. [SCENARIO] (cost_or_limit) If someone pushed this rule as far as it would go, where would it stop working for them?
5. [SCALE] (firmness) How strictly does this rule hold in your story? Scale: 1 = often bent or broken, 5 = never once broken.
6. [EITHER_OR] (who_knows) Is this rule common knowledge in your story's world, or understood by only a few? Options: common knowledge; understood by only a few.
7. [OPEN] (who_knows) What mistaken version of this rule, if any, do most people in your story's world believe?
8. [SCALE] (explanation_depth) How fully does the book set out this rule's workings for the reader? Scale: 1 = left a mystery, 5 = fully explained.
9. [SCENARIO] (reader_discovery) If a reader first met this rule at the moment it decides something important, what would they need to have seen earlier to accept it?
10. [OPEN] (everyday_impact) How does this rule show in the work and habits of people far from your story's main events?
11. [OPEN] (story_consequence) Which choice in your story could not happen the way it does without this rule?
12. [EITHER_OR] (story_consequence) Does this rule mostly open choices to your characters, or close them off? Options: opens choices; closes them off.
13. [SCENARIO] (cost_or_limit, story_consequence) If the cost of this rule were waived for one of your characters, what in your story would stop working?
14. [CHOICE] (continuity_risks) Where is a draft most likely to contradict this rule without meaning to? Options: in a hurried scene; when a character is cornered; when the cost is inconvenient; far from where the rule was first shown.
15. [SCALE] (rule_change) How different is this rule by the end of the book from how it first seemed? Scale: 1 = exactly as first shown, 5 = unrecognisable.
