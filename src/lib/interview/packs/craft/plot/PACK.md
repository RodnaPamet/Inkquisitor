# Plot

## Purpose

A plot entry records one plotline: whose choices drive it, the disturbance that sets it moving, the goal pursued in response, the complications that raise the pressure, the climax where the goal is won or lost, and what is settled afterwards. Good questioning helps the author state that chain of causes in their own words, find the links that are missing or loose, and leave with a spine their scenes can be checked against.

## How to question

- Never write the book's prose or supply story content: ask, summarise the author's own answers, and challenge them.
- Build any recommendation only from the author's stored entries and earlier answers, or from the options offered; when nothing supports one, offer none.
- Ask one thing per question: an obstacle and the owner's reaction to it are two questions.
- Follow the order the values rest on: the plotline owner and the opening disturbance first, then the driving goal, then the complications, then the climax, then the resolution. A climax asked before the goal exists has nothing to settle.
- Before building on a stored value, quote or restate it in a few words, so the author extends their own chain of events.
- To challenge a stored value, quote it and ask whether it still follows from the value before it or still leads to the one after; never ask for it afresh as if it were empty.
- Ask about cause as well as order: what each complication does to the goal, and why the next one follows from it.
- Keep to this plotline: the owner's inner life belongs to their character entry, single dated events to timeline event entries, the book's seed to the premise and its meaning to a theme entry.
- Keep every option craft-level: kinds of obstacle, directions, degrees of cost. An option never names a person, place, event or relationship the author has not given.
- When an earlier reply asks you to invent events or write a scene, do neither; leave that matter with the author and question what is still open.
- When nothing open is within reach of the depth dial, ask nothing and tell the author which values are waiting on which.

## Question patterns

### OPEN

- Suits the opening disturbance, the climax and the resolution once the detail dial allows a written reply.
- Ask what happens and why it happens in separate questions.
- Avoid wording that presumes an outcome, such as assuming the owner wins.

### CHOICE

- Good at low detail for the plotline's weight in the book, the kind of obstacle, where resistance comes from and where the goal ends up.
- For weight and outcome, base the options on the template's own; elsewhere offer three to five craft-level options that pull apart, and no catch-all.

### EITHER_OR

- Use it to make the author commit between two directions: an obstacle from outside the owner or from their own choice, a goal won or given up.
- Build both options from stored values or from a plain craft contrast; neither may be a straw option.
- Shape: "Does the next obstacle come from outside your plotline owner, or from a choice of their own?"

### SCENARIO

- Test the chain under a generic pressure: if one complication were removed, if the owner refused the goal, if the climax went the other way.
- Build the hypothetical only from stored values and earlier replies; never add an event the author has not given.
- Best for checking that the complications escalate and the climax follows from them, and for challenging a filled value.

### SCALE

- Suits degrees: how steeply the pressure rises, how evenly matched the opposition is, how much the resolution settles.
- State a plain meaning for 1 and for 5 every time, and use a scale only where the number folds back into the value it targets.

## Depth guidance

- **Depth 1** — The plotline owner, the opening disturbance and the plotline's weight are in reach. Keep to who drives the plotline and what breaks their starting situation, without hinting at how it ends.
- **Depth 2** — The driving goal comes into reach, with the plotline's span in the book. Ask what the owner sets out to achieve, put so that it can be won or lost.
- **Depth 3** — The complications come into reach, with the opposing force. Ask for obstacles one at a time, in story order, and how each makes the goal harder.
- **Depth 4** — The climax comes into reach, with the pace of escalation and the setups and payoffs. Test whether the climax follows from the stored complications and settles the stored goal.
- **Depth 5** — The resolution and the outcome of the goal come into reach. Ask what is settled and what has changed, then hold the resolution against the opening disturbance and probe what is left open on purpose.

## Never ask

- Never ask for anything already stored, or for anything asked, skipped or deferred earlier in this session.
- Never ask about a value whose prerequisites are still empty or deferred, whatever the depth dial allows.
- Leave the owner's wound, beliefs and inner change to their character entry; ask here only what they do.
- Leave the date and story time of single events to timeline event entries, and what the plotline means to theme entries.
- Never ask about sales prospects, the market, agents, publishers or publishing logistics.
- Never ask about the author's private life or the personal details of real people.
- Never ask which published book the plotline resembles, and never name one.

## Example questions

1. [OPEN] (plotline_owner) Whose choices drive this plotline, in the short phrase you would use to introduce them?
2. [CHOICE] (plotline_weight) How much of the book does this plotline carry? Options: main plot; major subplot; minor subplot; frame around the main story.
3. [OPEN] (opening_disturbance) What happens to break your plotline owner's starting situation and get this plotline moving?
4. [EITHER_OR] (opening_disturbance) Does the disturbance that starts this plotline arrive from outside your plotline owner, or come from a choice they make? Options: from outside them; from their own choice.
5. [OPEN] (driving_goal) What does your plotline owner set out to achieve in response to the disturbance, put so that it can be won or lost?
6. [SCENARIO] (driving_goal) If your plotline owner turned down that goal on the first day, what would follow from the disturbance?
7. [CHOICE] (opposing_force) Where does the main resistance to your plotline owner's goal come from? Options: another person; a group; the place or world around them; a circumstance nobody controls.
8. [OPEN] (complications) What is the first obstacle your plotline owner meets after committing to the goal?
9. [SCENARIO] (complications) If one complication on your list were taken out, which later obstacle would stop making sense?
10. [SCALE] (escalation_pace) How steeply should the pressure on your plotline owner rise from one complication to the next? Scale: 1 = a slow and gradual build, 5 = a steep and relentless climb.
11. [OPEN] (climax) What does your plotline owner do in the decisive confrontation where the goal is won or lost?
12. [EITHER_OR] (climax) Is the climax of this plotline decided by your plotline owner's own act, or by something done to them? Options: by their own act; by something done to them.
13. [CHOICE] (goal_outcome) Where does the climax leave your plotline owner's goal? Options: achieved; achieved at a heavy cost; lost; given up for a different aim; left open.
14. [OPEN] (resolution) Once the climax is over, what has changed for your plotline owner compared with the situation the disturbance broke?
15. [SCALE] (resolution) How much of this plotline does the resolution settle, rather than leave open? Scale: 1 = almost nothing settled, 5 = everything settled.
