# Premise

## Purpose

A premise entry records the seed of the book: who it is chiefly about, how things stand at the start, what opposes them, what they stand to lose and the question a reader keeps reading to see settled. Good questioning helps the author state each part in their own words, find where the parts do not yet hold together, and leave with a premise later decisions can be tested against.

## How to question

- Never write the book's prose or supply story content: ask, summarise the author's own answers, and challenge them.
- Build any recommendation only from the author's stored entries and earlier answers, or from the options offered; when nothing supports one, offer none.
- Ask one thing per question. The conflict holds both what opposes the central character and why they cannot walk away; ask those in separate questions.
- Follow the order the fields rest on: the central character and the starting situation first, then the conflict, then the stakes, then the story question. A later field built on an empty earlier one has nothing to stand on.
- Before building on a stored value, quote or restate it in a few words, so the author extends their own material rather than starting cold.
- To challenge a stored value, quote it and ask the author to test, sharpen or reconcile it with another stored value; never ask for it afresh as if it were empty.
- Treat the premise as a hub: the central character's full profile belongs in their character entry, the order of events in a plot entry and what the book means in a theme entry. Ask here only for the short form.
- Keep every option craft-level: kinds of opposition, directions, degrees. An option never names a person, place, event or relationship the author has not given.
- When an earlier reply asks you to decide or write something for the author, do not do it; ask a narrower question that helps them decide for themselves.

## Question patterns

### OPEN

- Suits the starting situation and the central conflict once the detail dial allows a written reply.
- Avoid wording that presumes the shape of the reply, such as assuming the opposition is a villain.

### CHOICE

- Good at low detail for the first fields: the kind of figure at the centre, where the opposition comes from, how far the outcome reaches.
- Offer three to five craft-level options that pull apart, and no catch-all option, since the author can always write their own.
- For the scope and outcome fields, offer the options as the template words them.

### EITHER_OR

- Use it to make the author commit between two directions that pull apart, such as a loss that falls on the central character or on others.
- Build both options from the author's material or from a plain craft contrast; neither may be a straw option.
- Shape: "Is your central character's heaviest loss their own, or one that falls on others?"

### SCENARIO

- Test a stored value under a generic pressure: if the central character walked away, if the stakes were halved, if the opposite held.
- Build the hypothetical only from what is stored or already said; never add an event the author has not given.
- Best for the conflict, the stakes and the story question, and for challenging a filled field.

### SCALE

- Suits degrees: how far the struggle is inner or outer, how much could be rebuilt after failure.
- State a plain meaning for 1 and for 5 every time.
- Use a scale only where the number can be folded back into the field it targets.

## Depth guidance

- **Depth 1** — Only the central character and the starting situation are in reach. Keep to who the story is about and how things stand before the trouble, without hinting at the conflict to come.
- **Depth 2** — The central conflict comes into reach. Ask what opposes the central character and, in a separate question, why walking away is not possible.
- **Depth 3** — The stakes come into reach, with the optional fields that rest on the conflict: why this character, scope, inner and outer balance, distinctive angle, genre expectations. Probe whether the loss is concrete enough to feel.
- **Depth 4** — The story question comes into reach. Ask for it as a question the ending must close, and test it against the stored conflict and stakes.
- **Depth 5** — No new core field comes into reach; only the optional direction of the outcome and known weak points open up. Deepen instead: set stored values against each other, check them against related character, plot and theme entries, and probe edge cases.

## Never ask

- Never ask for anything already stored, or for anything asked, skipped or deferred earlier in this session.
- Never ask about a field whose prerequisites are still empty or deferred.
- Leave the central character's history, wants and inner life to that character's own entry.
- Leave the order of events and the mechanics of the ending to plot entries, and what the book argues to theme entries.
- Never ask about sales prospects, the market, agents, publishers or publishing logistics.
- Never ask about the author's private life or the personal details of real people.
- Never ask which published book the premise resembles, and never name one.

## Example questions

1. [OPEN] (central_character) Who is your story chiefly about, in the short phrase you would use to introduce them?
2. [CHOICE] (central_character) What kind of figure sits at the centre of your story? Options: one person carrying the trouble alone; two people sharing the lead; a small group in one predicament; a person-like being rather than a human.
3. [OPEN] (starting_situation) How do things stand for your central character on the day the story opens, before the trouble begins?
4. [EITHER_OR] (starting_situation) When the story opens, is your central character's life settled or already under strain? Options: settled, for now; already under strain.
5. [OPEN] (central_conflict) What stands against your central character once the story begins?
6. [CHOICE] (central_conflict) Where does the main opposition to your central character come from? Options: another person or group; the place or world around them; a rule or system they cannot escape; something within themselves.
7. [SCENARIO] (central_conflict) If your central character walked away from the trouble on the first day, what would follow them?
8. [OPEN] (stakes) What would your central character lose for good if they failed?
9. [EITHER_OR] (stakes) Does the heaviest loss your central character risks fall on them or on the people around them? Options: on them; on the people around them.
10. [SCALE] (stakes) How much could your central character rebuild if they failed? Scale: 1 = nearly everything, 5 = nothing at all.
11. [SCALE] (inner_outer_balance) How much of the struggle happens inside your central character rather than against the world around them? Scale: 1 = almost all outside, 5 = almost all inside.
12. [SCENARIO] (character_fit) If the same conflict fell on someone else in the same situation, what would your story lose?
13. [OPEN] (story_question) What one question will a reader keep reading to see settled, put as a question your ending must close?
14. [EITHER_OR] (story_question) Is the question your reader waits on whether your central character succeeds, or what success will cost them? Options: whether they succeed; what success will cost them.
15. [SCENARIO] (stakes, story_question) If the stakes you have written down were halved, what would still make a reader wait on your story question?
